Damage-curve review · hail × real estate

# Hail × real estate curve, where its numbers come from

A hail curve for a building answers one question: **if hail of a given size hits it, what is the chance that the roof cover has to be replaced, and so what share of the building’s cost is lost?** It is built differently from hail and solar. There, laboratory counts of cracked panels gave dots to draw a line through.

Here the sources give two sizes for each roof, where damage starts and where it is general, and the Damage team chose to read those as a 5 % and a 95 % chance. So every dot sits at a published size, but its height is our judgment. The roof chance is then multiplied by the roof’s share of the building’s cost. A fourth, optional unit adds siding and windows, on numbers that are all chosen.

## Curve 1: house roof (laminated asphalt shingles, 10 years old)

Curve 1 · house roof: half replaced at 40 mm (about 1.6 in) · borrowed thresholds (T3) read by our judgment (T4)

**What each source says, and what that means for the line**

- **[#1 · Haag lab and field study, 2002](https://ams.confex.com/ams/pdfpapers/45858.pdf)** (numbers on the curve come from it)
  - ① 1.25 in (32 mm), field onset for laminated shingles: we chose 5 % ② 2.0 in (51 mm), every lab impact damaged: we chose 95 %
- **[#13 · Smith, IIBEC Interface 2013](https://iibec.org/publication-post/2013-02-smith/)** (numbers on the curve come from it)
  - laminated shingles 1¼ in: the same onset, from decades of tests and field inspections
- **Damage team, claim HRE-C020** (the Damage team’s own choice)
  - read onset as 5 % and full as 95 %, a smooth curve between (our judgment)

**On the Damage dashboard:** [1 · house roof](https://damage-curves-wsd6lcl64q-uc.a.run.app/cells/hail_real_estate/curves/HRE_RES_ROOF_ASPHALT_SHINGLE_LAMINATED)

This curve gives the chance that a house’s whole shingle roof has to be replaced, for the largest hailstone that hits it. Two sizes come from the sources, and both were re-read on their pages for this review.

Haag Engineering’s 2002 study (source [#1](https://ams.confex.com/ams/pdfpapers/45858.pdf)) gives 1.25 in (32 mm) as the field threshold for laminated shingles in its Table 3, and its Table 2 shows that 11-year-old laminated shingles were damaged by 0, 0, 60, 90 and 100 % of lab impacts at 1, 1.25, 1.5, 1.75 and 2.0 in; Smith’s 2013 table (source [#13](https://iibec.org/publication-post/2013-02-smith/)) gives the same 1¼ in onset.

The heights of the two dots are our judgment: the Damage team reads the onset as a 5 % chance and the size at which every lab impact damaged as a 95 % chance, with a smooth S-shaped curve between (claim HRE-C020).

The lab percentages are shares of impacts, not of roofs, and the paper says freezer ice is harder than real hail, so the cell keeps its curve at or below the lab points: 36 % at 1.5 in where 60 % of lab impacts did damage. The halfway point means that if hail of about 40 mm, a little over 1.5 in, hits the house, there is about an even chance the roof is replaced.

A new shingle roof is 3.75 % of the house’s replacement cost (Hazus roofing share 3 % times a repair factor 1.25, source [#6](https://www.fema.gov/sites/default/files/documents/fema_rsl_hazus-7-hutm_06272025_0.pdf)), about 15,200 dollars on the 405,000 dollar reference house, so at that size the house loses about 1.9 % of its value. The smallest size Hazard ever sends is 39 mm: every severe radar pixel arrives at least that large, already above the shingle onset.

The review (v5, A.15) finds the curve sound and says the yearly loss is set mostly by that floor, which is a Hazard-side choice.

## Curve 2: office roof (TPO membrane and insulation)

Curve 2 · office roof: half replaced at 36 mm · borrowed thresholds (T3) read by our judgment (T4)

**What each source says, and what that means for the line**

- **[#2 · Haag, Professional Roofing 2009](https://professionalroofing.net/articles/article/1506)** (numbers on the curve come from it)
  - ① 1 in (25 mm): we chose 5 % ② 2 in (51 mm): we chose 95 %; the single-ply range 1 to 2 in, read from a figure
- **#4 and #5 · SGH brief, IIBEC TPO tests** (numbers on the curve come from it)
  - single-ply damaged from 1.5 in; adhered TPO on a cover board survives 2 in ice balls (the low case)
- **Damage team, claim HRE-C020** (the Damage team’s own choice)
  - read the range as 5 % to 95 % (our judgment)

**On the Damage dashboard:** [2 · office roof](https://damage-curves-wsd6lcl64q-uc.a.run.app/cells/hail_real_estate/curves/HRE_OFF_ROOF_TPO_MEMBRANE_AND_INSULATION)

This curve gives the chance that the office’s roof membrane and the insulation under it are replaced. The sizes come from Haag’s low-slope threshold figure (source [#2](https://professionalroofing.net/articles/article/1506)): thermoplastic single-ply membranes are damaged by hail of 1 to 2 in, assuming hard hail, square-on impacts and a roof in fair condition.

That figure is an image and could not be read by this review’s fetch on 13 November, so the two dots are not yet re-read on the page; the article’s caption and assumptions were. The engineering firm SGH (source [#4](https://www.sgh.com/insight/understanding-hail-damage-risks-for-roofing-and-wall-systems/), re-read) puts single-ply damage at 1.5 in or more, inside the range. As for the house, the heights 5 % and 95 % are our judgment (claim HRE-C020).

The halfway point means that hail of about 36 mm gives an even chance of replacing the office roof, which is 2.7 % of the building’s cost (Hazus roof covering and insulation, with repair and overhead factors, source [#6](https://www.fema.gov/sites/default/files/documents/fema_rsl_hazus-7-hutm_06272025_0.pdf)).

Whether a real office sits low or high on this curve depends on how its membrane is fixed, which the reference profile does not say: an adhered membrane on a cover board survived 2 in ice balls in IIBEC’s tests (source [#5](https://iibec.org/publication-post/ice-ball-impact-resistance-of-heat-aged-tpo-roofing-membranes/)), and the cell keeps that as its low case.

## Curve 3: warehouse roof (standing-seam steel panels)

Curve 3 · warehouse roof: half replaced at 75 mm (about 3 in) · onset borrowed (T3), full size our judgment (T4)

**What each source says, and what that means for the line**

- **#2 and #3 · Haag 2009 and Haag 2010 via CLM** (numbers on the curve come from it)
  - ① 2.5 in (64 mm) needed to rupture steel panels in good to fair condition: we chose 5 %
- **Damage team, claim HRE-C021** (the Damage team’s own choice)
  - ② 3.5 in (89 mm): fully failed, no source (our judgment)

**On the Damage dashboard:** [3 · warehouse roof](https://damage-curves-wsd6lcl64q-uc.a.run.app/cells/hail_real_estate/curves/HRE_IND_ROOF_STANDING_SEAM_STEEL)

This curve gives the chance that the warehouse’s steel roof panels are replaced because hail ruptured them or opened their seams; dents alone do not count (decision D-HRE-05). One size is from the sources. CLM’s article on Haag’s 2010 ice-ball tests (source [#3](https://www.theclm.org/Magazine/articles/testing-your-mettle/1223), re-read) says hail of at least 2.5 in is typically needed to rupture metal panels in good to fair condition, and that dents on seam tops are not an open seam; Haag’s 2009 figure gives the same 2½ in for steel panels (source [#2](https://professionalroofing.net/articles/article/1506), figure not re-read).

That onset is read as 5 %, our judgment. The other dot is our judgment twice over: no source gives a size above 2.5 in, so the Damage team chose 3.5 in as the size at which the roof is fully failed (claim HRE-C021), with 3.0 and 4.0 in as the high and low cases. The roof’s share of the warehouse, 12 %, is also our judgment (claim HRE-C022).

The halfway point means that only hail of about 75 mm, roughly 3 in, gives an even chance of a new steel roof. Hazard’s radar size reaches that only when the raw radar estimate passes about 83 mm, so the warehouse roof is almost never hit; counting cosmetic dents instead would make it about as costly as the house (review, A.15).

## Curves 4a and 4b: walls and windows (an optional screening unit, all judgment)

Curves 4a and 4b · walls and windows: never reach half; at most 36 % of the house unit and 25 % of window units · our judgment (T4)

- **Curve 4a:** House · siding and windows
- **Curve 4b:** Office and warehouse · windows (the same line for both)

**What each source says, and what that means for the line**

- **Damage team, claim HRE-C023** (the Damage team’s own choice)
  - vinyl siding 1.25 to 2.0 in, half of it facing the hail; window glass 1.5 to 2.5 in, a quarter of panes reached (our judgment)
- **[#4 · SGH brief](https://www.sgh.com/insight/understanding-hail-damage-risks-for-roofing-and-wall-systems/)** (numbers on the curve come from it)
  - no hail rating exists for walls; glazing is susceptible; no size
- **[#7 · Hazard reference buildings](https://github.com/aamani-ai/Hazard_Modeling/blob/8b0d61c88fbcf85daa1c098516cee22398cdb524/docs/plans/_cross_cutting/real_estate_conus/reference_candidates.yml)** (numbers on the curve come from it)
  - wall and window areas; costs 7 dollars per sq ft of siding, 55 or 80 per sq ft of window are our judgment

**On the Damage dashboard:** [4a · house siding and windows](https://damage-curves-wsd6lcl64q-uc.a.run.app/cells/hail_real_estate/curves/HRE_RES_WALLS_WINDOWS_T4) · [4b · office windows](https://damage-curves-wsd6lcl64q-uc.a.run.app/cells/hail_real_estate/curves/HRE_OFF_WINDOWS_T4) · [4b · warehouse windows](https://damage-curves-wsd6lcl64q-uc.a.run.app/cells/hail_real_estate/curves/HRE_IND_WINDOWS_T4)

This unit adds broken siding and glass. No public test or field threshold exists for vinyl siding or building windows, and SGH’s brief (source [#4](https://www.sgh.com/insight/understanding-hail-damage-risks-for-roofing-and-wall-systems/), re-read) says so: there is no industry hail rating for wall coverings, and glazing is only described as susceptible. Every number here is therefore our judgment (claim HRE-C023).

Siding is given the shingle band, 1.25 to 2 in, with half of it on the walls facing the storm; window glass is given 1.5 to 2.5 in, with a quarter of the panes reached, because hail strikes walls at an angle and from one side. That is why the curves flatten out well below half: at most about 36 % of the house’s siding-and-window value and 25 % of a building’s windows.

The costs per square foot are also chosen, applied to the wall and window areas of the reference buildings (source [#7](https://github.com/aamani-ai/Hazard_Modeling/blob/8b0d61c88fbcf85daa1c098516cee22398cdb524/docs/plans/_cross_cutting/real_estate_conus/reference_candidates.yml)): the unit is 6.1 % of the house, 0.57 % of the warehouse and 5.3 % of the office. The curve has no halfway point. Hazard has to pass a named acknowledgement to use it at all (decision D-HRE-10); without it the unit is withheld, not zero.

In short: hail and real estate is three roof curves built from published hail sizes at which roofing starts to fail and fails everywhere, with the chance of replacement between those sizes chosen by us, times each roof’s share of the building’s cost. The house and office sizes and costs are published; the warehouse’s full-damage size and roof share, the 5 % and 95 % reading, and the whole walls-and-windows unit are our judgment. How much loss the curves produce in a year depends mostly on Hazard’s radar size, which never arrives below 39 mm. In the source table, the number of sources in each group is: C (dots we chose) 4, D (shape and choices) 5, E (bottom axis) 1, F (dollars) 2, I (background) 3, J (not used) 1.
